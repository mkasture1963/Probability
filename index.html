Experiential Learning of Probability - History

Three landmark repeated-trial investigations connect mathematical probability with observed evidence. Each historical experiment is considered through its theoretical setting, motivation, objective, procedure, recorded outcomes and interpretation.

1. Georges-Louis Leclerc, Comte de Buffon

1707–1788 · 18th-century Enlightenment · France
Portrait: Wikimedia Commons · public-domain artwork reproduction

Country: France

Specialization: Natural history, mathematics, biology, and scientific writing.

Probability contribution: Buffon's needle problem helped establish geometric probability; his coin-toss series became a celebrated early empirical illustration of long-run regularity.

Theoretical summary

For an ideal fair coin, the two outcomes have equal probability: P(H) = P(T) = 1/2. In repeated independent tosses, the number of heads X follows Binomial(n, 1/2), and the observed proportion X/n is expected to lie near 1/2 in a sufficiently long sequence. This is an empirical illustration of the Law of Large Numbers, established earlier by Jacob Bernoulli.

Motivation

To examine whether repeated observations of a chance event agree, in the long run, with the mathematical expectation of equal chances for heads and tails.

Objective

To compare the observed proportion of heads in a large series of coin tosses with the theoretical value 0.5, and to make random variation visible rather than expect exact equality.

Experiment conducted

A coin-tossing series traditionally attributed to Buffon, reported in the late eighteenth century, comprised 4,040 tosses. Historical retellings differ in how they describe who physically performed the tosses; therefore the result is best identified as Buffon's reported experiment rather than asserting that he personally made every toss.

Observed results

Heads: 2,048   |   Tails: 1,992

Observed relative frequency of heads:

2,048 / 4,040 = 0.50693 ≈ 50.69%

Observed relative frequency of tails: 0.49307 ≈ 49.31%

Conclusion

The observed proportion of heads was close to, but not exactly, 0.5. The result illustrates finite-sample fluctuation and is consistent with the theoretical model of a fair coin. Buffon's separate needle problem is a major landmark in geometric probability.

Reference & additional reading ↗

2. Karl Pearson

1857–1936 · Late 19th / early 20th century · United Kingdom
Photograph, 1910: Wikimedia Commons

Country: England, United Kingdom

Specialization: Mathematical statistics, biometrics, regression, and correlation.

Probability contribution: Developed the chi-square goodness-of-fit framework and made foundational contributions to correlation, regression and the systematic use of statistical methods.

Theoretical summary

Under the fair-coin model, each toss is a Bernoulli trial with p = 0.5. For n = 24,000, the expected number of heads is np = 12,000 and the standard deviation of the head count is √[np(1-p)] = √6,000 ≈ 77.46. A modest departure from 12,000 is therefore compatible with ordinary sampling variation.

E(X) = 12,000   ·   SD(X) = √6,000 ≈ 77.46

Motivation

To provide a large empirical series with which to examine the relationship between the mathematical expectation of a fair coin and the frequencies actually observed.

Objective

To record a substantial number of tosses, calculate the relative frequency of heads, and compare it with the theoretical probability 1/2.

Experiment conducted

Pearson's celebrated experiment, reported around 1900, comprised 24,000 coin tosses. The accumulated count was used as an illustration of how a large sample can yield a relative frequency close to the theoretical value.

Observed results

Heads: 12,012   |   Tails: 11,988

Observed relative frequency of heads:

12,012 / 24,000 = 0.5005 = 50.05%

Difference from theoretical 0.5: +0.0005 (0.05 percentage points).

Conclusion

The observed proportion, 0.5005, was very close to 0.5. The result is consistent with a fair-coin model; it does not prove fairness. Pearson's experiment is a striking classroom example of the distinction between theoretical probability and finite-sample relative frequency.

Reference & additional reading ↗

3. John Edmund Kerrich

1903–1985 · 1940s experiment · World War II
Portrait reproduced in University of Illinois probability lecture material.

Country / background: Born in England; grew up and later pursued an academic career in South Africa. The experiment was conducted while he was interned in Denmark.

Specialization: Mathematics, statistics, astronomy and actuarial mathematics.

Probability contribution: A systematic experimental exposition of probability laws, including the Law of Large Numbers and experiments with biased coins and urns.

Theoretical summary

For independent fair-coin tosses, X ~ Binomial(n, 0.5). The expected number of heads is n/2, while the observed proportion X/n fluctuates from one finite sample to another. The Law of Large Numbers states that this proportion converges in probability to 0.5 as n increases; it does not require heads and tails to alternate or be equal at every stage.

P(|X/n − 0.5| > ε) → 0 as n → ∞

Motivation

During wartime internment in Denmark, Kerrich used controlled, repeatable experiments to make fundamental probability ideas observable and to test how theoretical laws appeared in accumulated data.

Objective

To demonstrate experimentally the behaviour of relative frequencies over a long sequence, and to use recorded data to explain probability concepts to learners.

Experiment conducted

While interned at Hald Ege in Denmark during World War II, Kerrich worked with fellow internee Eric Christensen. They tossed a coin 10,000 times and recorded the cumulative outcomes. Kerrich later presented the work in An Experimental Introduction to the Theory of Probability (1946).

Observed results

Heads: 5,067   |   Tails: 4,933

Observed relative frequency of heads:

5,067 / 10,000 = 0.5067 = 50.67%

Standard error under p = 0.5: 0.005; deviation = 1.34 standard errors.

Conclusion

The proportion fluctuated substantially in the early trials and was closer to 0.5 over the long run. The final result is only 1.34 standard errors above 0.5, so it is not unusual under the fair-coin model. This is an empirical illustration—not a proof—of the Law of Large Numbers.

Reference & additional reading ↗
Interpretive caution: These are finite experimental records, not proofs that a particular coin is perfectly fair. The Law of Large Numbers concerns convergence of relative frequencies under stated probabilistic assumptions. Physical tossing technique and the coin's initial orientation can also affect outcomes.
